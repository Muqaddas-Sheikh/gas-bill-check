Gas Price Down But Bill Not Dropping

You hear that the gas price has gone down, yet when your bill arrives it looks almost the same as before, or even higher. It is a frustrating experience, and it leads many people to wonder whether the price cut was real at all. The short answer is that a gas price down but bill not dropping is a common situation, because a bill is made of several parts and the price of gas itself is only one of them. This guide explains, in general terms, why a lower gas price does not always mean a lower bill for a single household in a single month.

A Bill Is More Than the Gas Price

When people hear about a gas price change, they usually imagine the whole bill moving by the same amount. In reality, the price of gas is only one line in the bill. A typical household bill also includes fixed charges, taxes, and sometimes arrears or adjustments from earlier months. If the gas price falls but one of these other parts rises, or if your usage rises at the same time, the total can stay the same or even go up.

Think of it like a shopping bill. If the price of one item drops but you buy more items, or if a delivery charge is added, the total at the till may not fall. A gas bill works in a similar way: the total is the sum of several parts, and a change in one part does not guarantee a change in the total.

Fixed Charges Do Not Fall With the Gas Price

Fixed charges are set separately from the price of gas, and they do not automatically move when the gas price changes. These are the charges that apply to a connection regardless of how much gas is used, and they stay on the bill even in months of very low usage. If the fixed charge makes up a large share of your bill, a cut in the gas price will only affect the smaller, usage-based part of the total.

This effect is strongest for households that use little gas. If your monthly usage is small, the fixed charges may be larger than the gas-consumption charge itself. In that case, even a noticeable price cut changes only a small part of your bill, and the difference may be hard to see. Households with high usage feel a price change more, because the gas-consumption part of their bill is bigger.

Fixed charges can also change independently. If your consumer category was revised, for example from protected to non-protected, the fixed charge on your bill rises. That rise can easily cancel out the saving from a lower gas price, leaving the total unchanged or higher.

Your Usage May Have Risen at the Same Time

A price change rarely happens in isolation. If the price fell during a period when your household used more gas, the higher usage can hide the saving. A cold spell, guests staying over, a new appliance, or a geyser left running longer than usual can all raise the units on a bill. When units rise and price falls at the same time, the two effects pull in opposite directions, and the total may barely move.

To see whether this happened to you, compare the units consumed on the latest bill with the units on the bill before it, and with the same month last year. If the units went up, your usage is part of the story. If the units stayed the same or fell but the total did not, the reason lies in another part of the bill.

Arrears and Previous Balances Hide the Saving

Arrears are one of the most common reasons a bill stays high after a price cut. If part of an earlier bill was unpaid, or was paid late, that amount appears on the current bill. A late-payment surcharge may be added as well. When these older amounts sit on the bill, they can swallow the saving from the lower gas price entirely.

Look for lines on your bill marked as arrears, previous balance, or late payment charges. If you see them, check them against your payment receipts. Sometimes a payment made close to the due date reaches the company after the bill was prepared, so it appears as arrears on one bill and is adjusted on the next. In that case the saving from the price cut may show up a month later rather than immediately.

Reading Timing and Estimated Readings

The timing of meter readings matters more than most people realise. A bill covers a specific reading period, usually about a month. If a price change takes effect in the middle of a billing period, only part of that bill's usage may be charged at the new price, with the rest charged at the old price. The full effect of the change then appears only on the following bill.

Estimated readings can also blur the picture. If a bill was prepared on an estimate based on earlier, higher usage, the units on that bill may be higher than what you actually used. An estimate prepared before a price change, or one that spans the change, can make the bill look as though the price cut never happened. Once an actual reading is taken, the difference is normally adjusted in a later bill.

If you want to check this for yourself, read your own meter and compare it with the reading printed on the bill. A large gap between the two is a sign that the bill was estimated or that a reading needs correcting.

How to Check Whether the Price Cut Reached Your Bill

  • Compare the rate per unit on your latest bill with the rate on the previous bill. A lower rate confirms the change was applied to at least part of your usage.
  • Compare the units consumed. If units rose, higher usage may be hiding the saving.
  • Check for arrears, previous balance, and late payment charges that may be adding old amounts to the total.
  • Look for adjustment lines that belong to an earlier period.
  • Check whether the bill is based on an actual reading or an estimate, and compare the billed reading with your own meter reading.
  • Compare the fixed charges with the previous bill. A rise there can cancel out a price saving.
  • Wait for the next bill. If the price change fell in the middle of a billing period, the full effect may only appear on the following bill.
