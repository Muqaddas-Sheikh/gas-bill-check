RLNG vs LPG Pakistan

Many Pakistani households use gas every day, but not all of them use the same kind of gas. Some homes are connected to a pipeline, while others rely on cylinders delivered to the door. This comparison of rlng vs lpg pakistan explains both options in general terms: what piped RLNG is, what LPG cylinders are, where each is used, and the practical pros, cons, safety points, and availability issues of each, so you can understand which one suits which situation.

What Is RLNG?

RLNG stands for Re-gasified Liquefied Natural Gas. Natural gas is cooled into a liquid so it can be shipped in large quantities, and then turned back into gas before it is supplied to users. In Pakistan, RLNG is supplied through the pipeline network in areas where the distribution system reaches, and it is delivered to homes through the same kind of piped connection used for domestic natural gas.

For a household, RLNG behaves like ordinary piped natural gas: it arrives continuously through a metered connection, it is used for cooking, water heating, and room heating, and it is billed monthly by the gas distribution company. The two distribution companies in Pakistan are SNGPL, which serves Punjab, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Islamabad, and Azad Jammu and Kashmir, and SSGC, which serves Sindh and Balochistan. A household on RLNG receives a regular bill and a consumer number, just like any other piped-gas consumer.

What Is LPG?

LPG stands for Liquefied Petroleum Gas. Unlike piped gas, LPG is stored as a liquid under pressure in a cylinder and used as a gas when released through a regulator. Households buy cylinders from dealers or retailers, connect them to their stove or heater, and replace or refill the cylinder when it runs out. No pipeline connection is needed, which is what makes LPG useful in places the gas network does not reach.

LPG is used in homes, in small businesses such as restaurants and bakeries, and in vehicles in some cases. For a household, the most common use is cooking, though LPG heaters are also used in colder areas. Because the cylinder is portable, LPG can serve a home anywhere, from a city flat to a village house far from any pipeline.

Where Each Is Used

The choice between RLNG and LPG is often decided by location rather than preference. Piped gas, including RLNG, is available only where the distribution network exists. In cities and towns served by SNGPL or SSGC, many homes have piped connections. In areas without a pipeline, such as many rural areas and some newer housing schemes, LPG cylinders are the practical option.

Some households use both. A home with a piped connection may still keep an LPG cylinder as a backup for times when piped pressure is low or supply is interrupted. A home without a pipeline may use LPG for everything. Availability, therefore, is the first question to settle before comparing costs or convenience: if no pipeline reaches your street, LPG is the available option, and if a pipeline does reach you, a piped connection is usually the first choice to consider.

How They Compare: Convenience

Piped RLNG wins on day-to-day convenience. There is no cylinder to order, carry, or replace, and the supply is continuous as long as the network is running. You never run out of gas in the middle of cooking because a cylinder finished unexpectedly. Billing is monthly and usage is measured by the meter.

LPG requires more hands-on management. You must notice when a cylinder is running low, order a replacement, and be at home when it is delivered. A cylinder also takes up floor space and must be stored safely. On the other hand, LPG gives you control over when you buy: you pay for a cylinder when you need one, rather than receiving a monthly bill, which some households prefer for budgeting.

How They Compare: Cost in General Terms

Comparing costs is not straightforward, because the two are priced in different ways. Piped gas is billed per unit consumed, plus fixed charges and taxes, on a monthly bill. LPG is bought per cylinder, and the price of a cylinder changes with market conditions. A household comparing the two needs to look at its total monthly spending on each option, not at a single price figure.

In general, piped gas is often considered economical for regular household use, especially for cooking and water heating, because the household pays only for what it uses through the meter. LPG costs can add up when a household uses several cylinders a month, and the per-cylinder price can rise suddenly. However, a household that uses very little gas may find the fixed charges on a piped bill make the total less attractive. The honest answer is that the cheaper option depends on the household's usage level, local prices, and availability, and it is worth comparing your own monthly spending on both before deciding.

No specific current price is stated here, because LPG prices change frequently and piped-gas tariffs are revised from time to time. For the latest figures, check official company notices for piped gas and reputable retailer or official sources for LPG.

Safety Considerations

Both fuels are safe when handled properly, and both can be dangerous when handled carelessly. For piped gas, the main safety points are keeping the area around appliances ventilated, never using makeshift fittings, and reporting any smell of gas to the company immediately. Only authorised technicians should work on the pipeline or meter.

For LPG, safe storage and handling matter even more because the cylinder is inside the home. Cylinders should be kept upright, in a ventilated place, away from heat and direct flame. The regulator and hose should be checked regularly for wear or damage, and only standard, approved fittings should be used. A cylinder that is damaged, rusted, or past its inspection date should not be used. If you smell gas from a cylinder, do not switch on lights or appliances, ventilate the room, turn off the regulator if it is safe to do so, and move away from the area until the smell clears.

One practice to avoid entirely is using a suction device or compressor on a gas line to boost pressure. This is unsafe, can damage the network, and is against company rules. Our guide on gas compressor use and penalties explains why this is a problem and what to do instead when pressure is low.
